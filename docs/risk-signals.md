# OpenCheck — Risk Signals

All deterministic — every firing is documented with a `summary`, `confidence` (`high` / `medium` / `low`), and an `evidence` payload citing the underlying topic / collection / BODS statement IDs that triggered it.

Risk signals fall into three groups:

1. **Source-derived** — read straight off a single source's payload at search time.
2. **Structural complexity** — derived from the assembled BODS v0.4 bundle: layering, trusts and arrangements, nominees, and where the chain reaches. Regime-neutral by design; see the [regulatory crosswalk](#regulatory-crosswalk) for how they line up with the EU AMLA customer due diligence standards and others.
3. **Cross-source name match** — for every related person and entity inside the BODS bundle, search OpenSanctions and EveryPolitician by name (with optional birth-year compatibility) and surface a scoped signal on the matching node.

## Source-derived signals

- `PEP` — OpenSanctions `role.pep`-family topic, every EveryPolitician hit, or a Wikidata person with a currently-held position (P39 with no P582 end qualifier).
- `SANCTIONED` — OpenSanctions `sanction` topic: the record is itself the subject of a designation. Never fires on the adjacency subtopics below, and — since Phase 105 — never on `sanction.counter`.
- `COUNTER_SANCTIONED` — OpenSanctions `sanction.counter`: the record is designated under a counter-sanctions regime, which upstream defines as ["designations imposed by countries with weak democratic institutions … often punitive responses to foreign sanctions, or … used to suppress domestic political opposition"](https://www.opensanctions.org/docs/topics/). Structurally a direct listing of the record, and so reported at `high` confidence — the listing is a fact. What it is *not* is a designation by an authority the reader is likely to owe an obligation to, and appearing on such a list is frequently a consequence of journalism, sanctions enforcement or human-rights work. Rendered in slate at graph severity **2** — below plain adjacency, and deliberately outside the rose/amber sanctions colour ramp. Fires alongside `SANCTIONED` when a record carries both topics; neither substitutes for the other.
- `SANCTIONS_CONTROLLED` — OpenSanctions `sanction.control`: the record is a direct or **indirect** subsidiary, asset or vessel of a designated party. No percentage threshold and no depth limit are applied upstream (an end-dated shareholding stops the chain), so this is the starting point for an ownership-and-control test such as OFAC's 50 Percent Rule, not the answer to one. High confidence — the ownership assertion is deterministic; what is uncertain is the legal threshold, which the signal's summary states rather than discounting into the confidence level.
- `SANCTIONS_LINKED` — OpenSanctions `sanction.linked`: one-hop adjacency to a designated party (ownership, directorship, membership, employment, association, family, succession, or the company↔securities relationship). Upstream declares this topic a **superset** of `sanction.control`, so a controlled entity carries both and only `SANCTIONS_CONTROLLED` is surfaced. Any unrecognised `sanction.*` subtopic degrades to this signal — conservatively, and with a logged warning; `tests/test_opensanctions_live.py::test_sanction_family_is_fully_classified` fails the build when upstream adds one.
- `DEBARMENT` — OpenSanctions `debarment`: excluded from public contracts or procurement. A confirmed adverse listing, distinct from financial sanctions.
- `EXPORT_CONTROLLED` — OpenSanctions `export.control`: the record is itself subject to export-control restrictions (e.g. a BIS Entity List or Military End-User designation). *high* confidence. Added in Phase 118, after the `us_bis_mieu` dataset — all 13 of whose topic-bearing entities carry only this topic — was found to be fetched by search but classified into nothing.
- `EXPORT_CONTROL_LINKED` — OpenSanctions `export.control.linked`: adjacent to an export-controlled party; the record is not itself restricted. *medium* confidence. Unlike `sanction.linked` over `sanction.control`, upstream declares **no superset relationship** within the export family (verified against `model.json`, 2026-08-20), so nothing is suppressed when `EXPORT_CONTROLLED` also fires. Any unrecognised `export.*` subtopic degrades to this signal — conservatively, with a logged warning; `tests/test_opensanctions_live.py::test_export_family_is_fully_classified` fails the build when upstream adds one.
- `EXPORT_RISK` — OpenSanctions `export.risk` (upstream label "Trade risk"): flagged for trade-diversion risk, softer than a listing. *medium* confidence.

  Beyond the three families above, every topic in the adapter's `_RISK_TOPICS` must either map to a signal or appear on the explicit informational allowlist in `risk.py` (`_INFORMATIONAL_TOPICS` — 18 topics today: `poi`, `wanted`, `role.oligarch`, the `crime.*` family, `invest.*`, `mare.*`, `reg.*`, `corp.disqual`). `tests/test_opensanctions_live.py::test_every_risk_topic_is_classified_or_allowlisted` enforces the partition, so "we fetch it but don't understand it" is a build failure rather than a discovery — promoting an allowlisted topic to a signal is a deliberate edit with its own ticket.
- `OFFSHORE_LEAKS` — a name in the BODS bundle matches a record in the ICIJ Offshore Leaks database (Panama Papers, Paradise Papers, Pandora Papers, Bahamas Leaks, Offshore Leaks) via the ICIJ reconciliation API; or an OpenAleph hit in an ICIJ-family collection (OpenAleph is currently disabled in `REGISTRY` but this signal also fires via the ICIJ name cross-check, which requires no API key).
- `OPAQUE_OWNERSHIP` — a party exists whose identity is deliberately withheld or could not be obtained; every firing is a claim the register itself makes (*high* confidence). Three families:
  - **GLEIF `NON_PUBLIC`-family reporting exceptions** — the entity declines to name a known accounting-consolidation parent (`NON_PUBLIC` plus the five deprecated pre-2022 reasons `BINDING_LEGAL_COMMITMENTS`, `LEGAL_OBSTACLES`, `DISCLOSURE_DETRIMENTAL`, `DETRIMENT_NOT_EXCLUDED`, `CONSENT_NOT_OBTAINED`). Classified from the raw exception record's reason code, per the [GLEIF Level 2 Reporting Exceptions 2.1 format](https://www.gleif.org/en/lei-data/access-and-use-lei-data/level-2-data-reporting-exceptions-2-1-format).
  - **`anonymousPerson` / `anonymousEntity` statements** — identifying details deliberately withheld, e.g. a Companies House super-secure PSC protected by court order.
  - **Unspecified-`interestedParty` relationships** whose reason is `subjectUnableToConfirmOrIdentifyBeneficialOwner` or `interestedPartyHasNotProvidedInformation` — the register says an owner exists but is unidentified or non-cooperative (`noBeneficialOwners` and `interestedPartyExemptFromDisclosure` deliberately do not fire).

  Deliberately does **not** fire on `unknownPerson` / `unknownEntity` statements: unknown-to-this-source is a different claim from deliberately-withheld, and the GLEIF reporting-exception bridges use exactly those types for the benign exception reasons below. GLEIF Level 2 only ever names *entities* (LEI holders), so no wording anywhere claims an "unknown person in the ownership chain" on GLEIF evidence.
- `GLEIF_REPORTING_EXCEPTION` — **context, not risk** (`kind: "context"`, *high* confidence). A GLEIF Level 2 reporting exception from the benign/structural family, i.e. the [permitted reasons under the LEI ROC policy](https://www.gleif.org/en/lei-data/access-and-use-lei-data/level-2-data-reporting-exceptions-2-1-format) for having no accounting-consolidation parent record:
  - `NATURAL_PERSONS` — controlled directly by natural person(s) with no intermediate consolidating legal entity (common for founder-, family-owned **and** widely held companies — Eli Lilly reports this for both categories);
  - `NO_KNOWN_PERSON` — no known controlling person (diversified shareholding);
  - `NON_CONSOLIDATING` — controlling entities not subject to preparing consolidated financial statements;
  - `NO_LEI` — a parent exists but does not consent to have an LEI, so it is not identified in GLEIF (distinct wording in the summary: a real transparency gap in GLEIF coverage, but national registers may still identify the parent — not evidence of opacity).

  Unrecognised future reason codes fall into this context signal rather than the risk one. The evidence carries each exception's `category` (`DIRECT_ACCOUNTING_CONSOLIDATION_PARENT` / `ULTIMATE_ACCOUNTING_CONSOLIDATION_PARENT`), `reason`, `reference` (the legal basis, when supplied) and the bridging statement's `statement_id` for graph badges.

## Geographic risk — the standing rule

**OpenCheck emits a geographic *risk* signal only where an authoritative, externally maintained, dated list says so.** Today that means the FATF lists and the EU's Article 29 list, below. Everything else about where an ownership chain reaches is reported as **context** (`kind: "context"`), not risk.

This is not conservatism for its own sake — it follows the framework. AMLR **Annex III(3)** enumerates higher-risk geography as *qualified* categories of third country (FATF-listed, no effective AML/CFT system, significant corruption, sanctioned, terrorist financing, financial secrecy), and **Annex II** places third countries with effective AML/CFT systems in the **lower**-risk column. "Being a third country" is on neither list.

Two things were considered and deliberately **not** built:

- **A jurisdiction-level signal for Annex III(3)(f)(iv)** — "not requiring beneficial ownership information to be recorded or held in a central database or register". Three problems: the ground truth moves faster than any list we could ship (FinCEN removed US domestic BO reporting in August 2026); the criterion turns on whether information is *recorded*, not published, so a register open to law enforcement but closed to the public **satisfies** it while being invisible to a public-data tool; and the only observation actually available to us — "we found no BO data" — conflates *no register exists*, *the register is closed*, and *OpenCheck has no adapter for it yet*. The third case is our coverage gap, and shipping it as jurisdiction risk would turn that gap into an accusation against a country. Where OpenCheck genuinely observes an absence, that is entity-scoped and `OPAQUE_OWNERSHIP` already models it.
- **An Annex II lower-risk allowlist** — deciding which third countries have "effective AML/CFT systems" means adjudicating FATF mutual-evaluation outcomes that are 5–10 years old and rated across 40 Recommendations and 11 Immediate Outcomes. That is an editorial position to defend and maintain, in order to suppress a chip that is no longer emitted.

### Signal kinds

Every signal carries `kind`: `"risk"` (an adverse finding) or `"context"` (a structural observation). The classification lives on the signal, not in per-surface exclusion lists, because the risk **count** is rendered independently by the results page, the OG share card and the share-page meta description. A missing `kind` is read as `"risk"`, so payloads cached before the field existed behave as before.

## Jurisdiction list signals

**Scope (Phase 273): the subject and the ownership chain above it — nothing else.** Each list signal reads the looked-up company (every entity statement carrying its identifier — MEIP, for one, files a statement per group membership) and its owners, transitively upwards. A hit on the subject is said as its registration (*"The company is registered in Iran, …"*); a hit above it as *"Its ownership chain reaches into …"*; both together as *"The company is registered in Kenya, and its ownership chain reaches into Iran, …"*. Each hit in `evidence.jurisdictions[]` carries `position` (`"subject"` / `"above"`). Subsidiaries go to `SUBSIDIARY_LISTED_JURISDICTION` (context, below); side branches — entities neither above nor below the subject, such as a parent's other subsidiaries — are reported nowhere. Ended links follow Phase 220: an owner reached only through an ended relationship still counts, the hit carries `via_ended_only: true`, and the sentence says *"including ended relationships"* while some country is reached that way alone (`evidence.ended_relationship_statement_ids`).

Until Phase 273 these signals walked **every** entity statement in a bundle — the defect Phase 153 fixed for `NON_EU_JURISDICTION` and Phase 170 for the layer count. GLEIF bundles list a parent's direct children, so the chip and the sentence "ownership chain reaches into …" usually described a subsidiary. Measured on 30 production subjects on 1 Oct 2026, 26 of 33 list-signal firings rested on subsidiaries alone, and 12 companies (TotalEnergies, Unilever, Siemens, Maersk, HSBC, BAT, Orange, Société Générale, Heineken, Bolloré, Holcim, Danone) carried FATF / EU chips — and for 10 of them the verdict's "a jurisdiction on an international watch list" — on nothing else.

- `FATF_BLACK_LIST` — the subject or an owner registered in a FATF High-Risk Jurisdiction subject to a Call for Action. *high* confidence. Update at each plenary (typically February, June, October).
- `FATF_GREY_LIST` — the subject or an owner registered in a FATF Jurisdiction under Increased Monitoring. *medium* confidence. A black-listed country never also fires grey.
- `SUBSIDIARY_LISTED_JURISDICTION` — `kind="context"`, *low*. Subsidiaries (entities below the subject, transitively) registered in a jurisdiction on any of the three lists, each hit naming every list it is on (`evidence.jurisdictions[].lists`: `eu`, `fatf_black`, `fatf_grey`; not narrowed by `OPENCHECK_HIGH_RISK_JURISDICTION_LISTS`). *"Subsidiaries registered in listed jurisdictions: Kenya (EU high-risk list, FATF grey list), Russian Federation (EU high-risk list). Structural context, not a risk finding …"* A subsidiary's jurisdiction is not the company's ownership chain, but it is not nothing — TotalEnergies' Russian hits are Arctic LNG 2, Société Générale's is Rosbank — so it is reported, never counted as risk and never in the verdict. One note per lookup: it collapses globally with a pooling resolver (`merge_subsidiary_listed_jurisdiction`, on the same terms as `NON_EU_JURISDICTION`), so every subsidiary node keeps its slate `Sj` graph badge while owners keep the red `F!` / `Fg` / `EU!`. A former subsidiary still counts, qualified *"including ended relationships"*.
- `EU_HIGH_RISK_THIRD_COUNTRY` — the subject or an owner registered in a jurisdiction on the Annex to **Commission Delegated Regulation (EU) 2016/1675**, as amended. *high* confidence, because unlike a FATF listing an EU designation is a binding trigger for mandatory enhanced due diligence rather than an international assessment.

  Kept as a **separate code** from the FATF signals, not a widening of them. The two instruments diverge in practice, because the Commission adopts its updates months after the FATF plenary that prompted them — as of the current lists, Algeria and Namibia are EU-listed but were removed from the FATF grey list in June 2026, Myanmar is FATF black but sits in the EU's Section I, and Bosnia and Herzegovina, Bulgaria, Iraq, Kuwait and Papua New Guinea are FATF grey but not EU-listed. Folding them together would leave the summary unable to say which instrument applies, and a delisting from one would silently move a signal attributed to the other.

  **Section IV — the Russian Federation.** Delegated Regulation (EU) 2026/46 inserted a fourth section into the Annex for "high-risk third countries which are not identified as being subject to calls for action or increased monitoring by the FATF, but whose membership in that international standard-setter is suspended", and listed Russia in it on grounds including FIU independence, beneficial ownership transparency and the AML/CFT treatment of crypto-assets. Russia is on **no** FATF list — its membership was suspended on 24 February 2023 following the invasion of Ukraine, with follow-up running through the Eurasian Group. This is the widest case of the divergence above: the EU listing rests on a category the FATF does not have.

  It is deliberately **not** a separate signal code. Article 29 attaches the same mandatory enhanced due diligence to every jurisdiction in the Annex regardless of section, so a distinct code would imply a distinct obligation, fragment the severity ladder, and need its own chip, colour, graph severity and share-card label. The section is a property of the hit — `evidence.jurisdictions[].annex_section`, `"I-III"` or `"IV"` — and a Section IV hit appends a clause to the signal's sentence naming the suspension, so Russia is never reported as a jurisdiction the FATF identified as deficient. Sections I–III need no equivalent: each tracks a FATF listing, so the FATF signals already carry the nuance alongside this one.

  **Refresh gotcha.** The list is amended by more than one instrument at a time. (EU) 2026/46 and (EU) 2026/83 were adopted a day apart, published in the same OJ issue and apply from the same date, so `EU_HRTC_INSTRUMENT`'s date stayed correct while the list was a country short for seven months. Check the **amending-act list** on EUR-Lex, not the date — and never a secondary AML tracker: two well-known ones were found in August 2026 serving a list three years out of date.

## Structural complexity signals (BODS v0.4 derived)

OpenCheck's own indicators of how complex an ownership structure is. Each describes something observable in the BODS graph and fires as its own chip. **None asserts that a legal threshold has been met**, and since Phase 272 the chip sentences name no regulator: the same facts matter under the EU AMLA standards, FATF Recommendation 24 guidance and national regimes alike, and OpenCheck increasingly covers registers outside the EU. How they map to specific frameworks is in the [crosswalk](#regulatory-crosswalk) below.

- `TRUST_OR_ARRANGEMENT` — entity with `entityType=arrangement` or a legal-form keyword (`trust`, `Stiftung`, `Anstalt`, `fideicomiso`, `Treuhand`, `foundation`).
- `NON_EU_JURISDICTION` — `kind="context"`. An entity **on the ownership chain above the subject** (an interested party of the subject, or of one of its owners, transitively) whose `recordDetails.jurisdiction.code` (BODS v0.4; falls back to a top-level `incorporatedInJurisdiction` dict for v0.3-shaped pass-through fixtures) is outside the EU+EEA. The subject's own jurisdiction is never counted — where a company *is* is not somewhere its chain *reaches* — and neither are subsidiaries below it. A bundle with no owners in it raises nothing. Since Phase 153; before that the rule walked every entity statement in the bundle. Configurable via `OPENCHECK_AMLA_EQUIVALENT_JURISDICTIONS` (additive, e.g. `GB,CH`) or `OPENCHECK_AMLA_EU_EEA_OVERRIDE` (full replace) — legacy names, kept for existing deployments.

  **A structural observation, never an adverse finding, and since Phase 272 an input to nothing.** Being outside the EU/EEA is not a risk factor in itself: AMLR Annex III(3) lists only *qualified* categories of third country, and Annex II puts third countries with effective AML/CFT systems in the **lower**-risk column. Until Phase 272 non-EU status on the layered path was one condition of the `COMPLEX_CORPORATE_STRUCTURE` composite, mirroring the consultation draft of AMLA's CDD RTS; the final draft replaced "registered outside the EU" with "registered in high-risk jurisdictions", which OpenCheck answers from the FATF and EU lists. For jurisdiction *risk*, see `FATF_GREY_LIST` / `FATF_BLACK_LIST` / `EU_HIGH_RISK_THIRD_COUNTRY`.

  Merged into one note per lookup (Phase 259), so every non-EU node found by every source keeps its graph badge.

### Dedup: which codes collapse globally

`_merge_signals` in `routers/lookup.py` collapses "structural" codes on `(code,)` alone — and because it assigns rather than combines, only the last-processed source's evidence survives. Membership of that set is therefore **not** "is this a structural claim?" but a narrower test: **does the signal's evidence identify particular nodes?**

`TRUST_OR_ARRANGEMENT`, `NOMINEE` and `NON_EU_JURISDICTION` all carry per-node `statement_id`s (in `evidence.matches[]` / `evidence.jurisdictions[]`) that the graph reads to draw badges, so they dedup **per source**. `COMPLEX_OWNERSHIP_LAYERS`, `POSSIBLE_OBFUSCATION` and `SANCTIONED_SECURITY` are whole-structure claims (as was the retired `COMPLEX_CORPORATE_STRUCTURE`) and still collapse globally.

`COMPLEX_OWNERSHIP_LAYERS` must **not** be moved out — per source it would report conflicting layer counts, and the chip strip picks its winner by confidence rather than depth, so the number shown would be arbitrary. It stays globally collapsed and resolves the conflict with a **depth resolver**: the deepest chain wins, so the reported depth no longer depends on which source happened to be processed last, and the surviving `longest_path` is the chain that justifies the number.

That resolver is exactly equivalent to computing the depth over the merged bundle, because statement ids are namespaced per source (`_stable_id(source_id, …)`) — no ownership edge ever bridges two sources, so `bods_all` is a concatenation of disjoint subgraphs and its longest path *is* the maximum of the per-source longest paths.
- `NOMINEE` — a nominee shareholder or director arrangement. Two grades of evidence, reported distinctly:
  - **structured** (*high* confidence) — the register filed a nominee code. Companies House / Register of Overseas Entities `natures_of_control` codes matching `registered-owner-as-nominee-*` (six of them; see `bods/psc_natures.NOMINEE_NATURE_CODES`). The code travels in the signal's evidence so a reviewer can check the filing rather than our reading of it. Ceased PSCs are excluded — that arrangement has ended.
  - **textual** (*medium* confidence) — the word "nominee" (or `prête-nom` / `fiduciaire` / camelCase variants) appears in an interest type, an interest's details, or a person record. Real evidence, but weaker than a filed code: "Nominee Services Ltd" is a company name, not a declaration. The summary says outright that it matched on descriptive text.

  `evidence.basis` is `"structured"` or `"textual"`. A filed code reports as structured even though the mapper also renders it into `interest.details` — the same fact must not be reported by its weaker trace.
- `COMPLEX_OWNERSHIP_LAYERS` — `medium`. The chain of corporate layers **above the subject** has **two or more intermediate layers** (three or more entity nodes counting the subject; cycle-safe DFS). The bar is an OpenCheck display threshold (`LAYERS_MIN_ENTITIES = 3`), not a legal one — no framework OpenCheck tracks sets one. It sits where the final draft RTS begins asking for per-layer information (Art 11(2): "more than one legal entity or legal arrangement between the customer and their beneficial owners").

  Since Phase 272 the signal also records **which other complexity elements sit on that chain**, in `evidence.complexity_elements[]`, and names them in its sentence — e.g. *"Ownership chain above the subject has 3 intermediate corporate layers. Also present: an entity on the chain registered in a high-risk jurisdiction (VG) and nominee arrangements in the structure."* Each entry has an `element`:

  | `element` | Scope | Evidence |
  |---|---|---|
  | `arrangement` | entities on `longest_path` only ("in any of those layers") | `statement_ids[]` |
  | `high_risk_jurisdiction` | entities on `longest_path`, **subject included** ("the customer or any legal entities present at any of these layers") | `jurisdictions[]` with `statement_id`, `code`, `name`, `lists[]` |
  | `nominee` | **bundle-wide**, deliberately ("involvement … in the structure" is looser wording) | `scope: "structure"`, `basis` (`structured` / `textual`) |

  **"High-risk jurisdiction"** is undefined in the final draft RTS. OpenCheck reads it from the dated lists it already maintains — the EU Article 29 list (`eu`), the FATF black list (`fatf_black`) and the FATF grey list (`fatf_grey`) — all three by default, narrowable with `OPENCHECK_HIGH_RISK_JURISDICTION_LISTS`. That is the standing geographic-risk rule: no geographic risk claim without an authoritative, externally maintained, dated list. Each hit says which lists it is on.

  The layers that count are those "**between the customer and the beneficial owner**" (the AMLA RTS wording, consultation and final drafts alike), and until Phase 170 the walk honoured neither half of that phrase. It started from every entity node in the bundle and kept the longest path found *anywhere*, so a chain among parties that never touch the subject satisfied the rule on the subject's behalf — in production on 2026-09-05, Shell plc's chip and its verdict sentence ("over an ownership chain 3 layers deep") rested on `BlackRock → Royal Dutch Shell plc → Shell Midstream Operating LLC`, three nodes none of which is the looked-up Shell plc. And because BODS edges run `interestedParty --(owns)--> subject`, following them forwards walked **downwards into subsidiaries**: a GLEIF bundle listing a parent's children reported the depth of the group *below* the subject as the depth of the chain *above* it. Same defect, same fix as `_non_eu_jurisdiction_signal` in Phase 153 — the walk is anchored at `_subject_entity_id` and follows `subject → interestedParty` upwards, entity nodes only.

  `evidence.layers` **includes the subject** — the number the depth resolver and `graph_shape.depth` read. Since Phase 272 everything a reader sees reports `evidence.intermediate_layers` (`layers − 1`) instead — the chip's sentence, the verdict ("Its ownership chain has 3 intermediate corporate layers", `VERDICT_TEMPLATE` 4, so the watchlist does not report the rewording as a change) and the verdict strip's network column (`graph_shape.intermediate_layers`, falling back to `depth − 1` on runs saved earlier) — because the final draft RTS speaks of *intermediate* layers and every entity above the subject is one (a beneficial owner is a natural person). The count is a **lower bound**, not a measured distance to a named BO: a chain running four entities up without reaching a person still has at least those four layers under whoever the BO turns out to be, so no person terminus is required — which also keeps the signal alive on GLEIF and OpenCorporates bundles, neither of which carries person statements. `evidence.reaches_beneficial_owner` records whether one was actually found, so a consumer can tell a completed chain from a truncated one.
- `POSSIBLE_OBFUSCATION` — advisory (`low`), labelled "Opacity with layering". Fires when `OPAQUE_OWNERSHIP` (a withheld or unidentified party) and `COMPLEX_OWNERSHIP_LAYERS` are both present. Whether a structure has a legitimate rationale cannot be judged from data, and the sentence says so, pointing to an analysis of that rationale as an enhanced due diligence step. Until Phase 272 it mirrored the consultation draft's subjective condition (d) and also fired on layers plus a non-EU entity; the final draft moved the question out of CDD into an optional EDD measure (Art 21(d)), and non-EU status no longer counts towards anything. The code is unchanged so stored reports and watch snapshots stay comparable.

### Retired: `COMPLEX_CORPORATE_STRUCTURE`

Until Phase 272 a `high` composite fired when `COMPLEX_OWNERSHIP_LAYERS` and at least two of {trust on the path, non-EU entity on the path, nominee} were present, with the sentence *"Meets AMLA CDD RTS threshold for a complex corporate structure"*. It mirrored old Article 12(1) of the February 2026 consultation draft. The [final draft](https://www.amla.europa.eu/document/download/e15ab2e5-4074-4de8-bb9d-613dd8b7fdd1_en?filename=Final%20Report%20-%20draft%20RTS%2028%281%29%20AMLR.pdf) (30 Sep 2026) dropped the definition and the threshold — AMLA said it had moved away from a prescriptive definition that automatically triggered mandatory measures — so a chip saying a structure "meets" it would assert a rule that does not exist. The engine no longer emits the code. It stays in `risk.RETIRED_SIGNAL_CODES` so that saved reports, batch rows and watchlist snapshots written earlier still render (as "Complex structure (withdrawn draft rule)", in the quiet context palette), and so the watchlist does not report its disappearance as a retired finding.

## Regulatory crosswalk

How the structural signals line up with the frameworks that look at the same facts. This mapping is documentation, not a claim the signals make: chip sentences stay regime-neutral.

| OpenCheck signal / element | EU — AMLA CDD RTS, final draft (Art 28(1) AMLR, 30 Sep 2026)* | EU — consultation draft (Feb 2026), for history |
|---|---|---|
| `COMPLEX_OWNERSHIP_LAYERS` (intermediate layers) | Art 11(4)(a) "the number of intermediate layers between the customer and the beneficial owner"; Art 11(2) per-layer information where more than one intermediate entity | Old Art 12(1): "three or more layers" |
| `complexity_elements: arrangement` / `TRUST_OR_ARRANGEMENT` | Art 11(4)(b) legal arrangements or similar legal entities "in any of those layers" | Old Art 12(1)(a) |
| `complexity_elements: high_risk_jurisdiction` / `FATF_*`, `EU_HIGH_RISK_THIRD_COUNTRY` | Art 11(4)(c) customer or layer entity "registered in high-risk jurisdictions" (term undefined; OpenCheck reads the EU Art 29 and FATF lists) | Old Art 12(1)(b) "outside the EU" — no longer used |
| `complexity_elements: nominee` / `NOMINEE` | Art 11(4)(d) "involvement of nominee shareholders or nominee directors in the structure"; Art 11(2)(b) | Old Art 12(1)(c) |
| `POSSIBLE_OBFUSCATION` | Art 21(d) (EDD): analysis or expert opinion on the rationale of a structure where its complexity contributes to high risk | Old Art 12(1)(d) (CDD condition) |
| `NON_EU_JURISDICTION` | — (context only) | Old Art 12(1)(b) |
| `COMPLEX_CORPORATE_STRUCTURE` (retired) | — no definition or threshold | Old Art 12(1) composite |

\* Still a *final draft*: the Commission can amend it before adoption. Re-check on adoption. Further frameworks (FATF Recommendation 24 guidance, national regimes) belong as further columns as OpenCheck's coverage grows.

## Ownership structure (BODS v0.4 derived)

- `STATE_CONTROLLED` — `medium` — a party that owns or controls the subject (directly or further up — never a state that owns one of its subsidiaries) is modelled as a `state` or `stateBody` entity, i.e. the subject connects to a state per the BODS [Representing state-owned enterprises](https://standard.openownership.org/en/0.4.0/standard/modelling/repr-state-owned-enterprises.html) requirement — a possible state-owned enterprise. Source-agnostic; the state nodes come from Wikidata's controlling owners (`P127`/`P749`, classified through `P279*` since Phase 240), GEM's Entity Type column, the EITI SOE database, OpenSanctions' `PublicBody` schema and government topics, and — for any source — an owner whose LEI GLEIF files as a `RESIDENT_GOVERNMENT_ENTITY` (read from the Golden Copy mirror, `bods/state_bodies.py`). **One signal per lookup, one holding per state** (Phase 240): the parties are grouped by the state's ISO code, so Equinor reads *Norway, 67% — FINANSDEPARTEMENTET (OpenSanctions); Ministry of Trade, Industry and Fisheries (Wikidata)* rather than two 67% holders. The bodies are grouped as the same state, never asserted to be the same body; every node keeps its badge. **Presence-only and corroborating**: every source covers part of the world, so the signal's *absence is not evidence* an entity is privately owned, and it is never a determination. Not a structural complexity element. Evidence carries `matches[]` (one entry per state party: `statement_id`, `name`, `source_id`, `state`, `share`, `current`, `direct`), `state_holdings[]` (per state: `shares`, `sources`, `current`, `party_statement_ids`), `sources`, and the anchor node (`statement_id`) and controlled entity (`subject_statement_id`). A state with no current holding adds *including ended relationships*. See [docs/wikidata-ownership.md](wikidata-ownership.md).

## FATF jurisdiction signals (BODS v0.4 derived)

For the subject and every entity on the ownership chain above it (Phase 273 — see [Jurisdiction list signals](#jurisdiction-list-signals) for the scope and the subsidiary note), OpenCheck checks `recordDetails.jurisdiction.code` against the FATF lists current as of the June 2026 plenary (refreshed each FATF plenary: typically February, June, and October). Two independent signals, with different confidence levels reflecting FATF's own severity distinction:

- `FATF_BLACK_LIST` — `high` — entity in the FATF High-Risk Jurisdictions (Call for Action) list: **Democratic People's Republic of Korea (KP), Iran (IR), Myanmar (MM)**.
- `FATF_GREY_LIST` — `medium` — entity in the FATF Jurisdictions under Increased Monitoring list (June 2026 plenary): Angola, Bolivia, Bosnia and Herzegovina, Bulgaria, Cameroon, Côte d'Ivoire, Democratic Republic of Congo, Haiti, Iraq, Kenya, Kuwait, Laos, Lebanon, Monaco, Nepal, Papua New Guinea, South Sudan, Syria, Venezuela, Vietnam, British Virgin Islands, Yemen.

Both signals are derived purely from the BODS jurisdiction codes — they fire independently of the structural signals (which also read them, for the `high_risk_jurisdiction` complexity element) and require no additional source calls. The country code sets live in `risk.py` (`FATF_BLACK_LIST_CODES` / `FATF_GREY_LIST_CODES`) and should be updated after each FATF plenary.

## Cross-source name match

For every `personStatement` and `entityStatement` in the assembled BODS bundle, OpenCheck searches OpenSanctions (and EveryPolitician for persons) by name. Matches above a similarity threshold of 0.88 — with optional birth-year compatibility (±1 year, only when both sides supply a DOB) — produce **scoped** signals attached to the matching related-party's `statementId` (in `evidence.subject_statement_id`), not the subject. That means a sanctioned PSC behind an otherwise clean shell company surfaces on the right node in the graph.

**The looked-up company is never its own related party (Phase 235).** The bundle holds every source's statement *of the subject* as well as its related parties — OpenSanctions' record for Rosneft carries Rosneft's own LEI — and screening those produced the subject's own listing a second time, worded as third-party exposure (`RELATED_SANCTIONED` on Rosneft; "Related entity 'CLP HOLDINGS LIMITED'" on CLP Holdings). `opencheck/subject_identity.py` builds the **subject identity set** once per lookup: the statements `subject_profile.subject_statements` names, plus every entity statement sharing an identifier with them (the LEI, a register number, a source's own record id) — **never a name**. The OpenSanctions/EveryPolitician, OpenAleph and ICIJ screens leave that set out of their related-party targets. A statement joined to the subject by a relationship is a counterparty, not the subject, and stays in the screen whatever identifier it shares, unless it too carries the looked-up LEI. The subject is still screened, at subject level: by the LEI-keyed OpenSanctions and OpenAleph adapters, and — ICIJ having no such adapter — by the ICIJ check itself, below. The register hop in `/expand-layer` passes no LEI and screens its hop node as before.

### Corroboration: what a person match rests on

A name match is weak evidence about a *person*, and the scorer cannot be tuned out of it. `Michael R. Gordon` and `Michael E. Gordon` — definitively different people — score **0.9375**, above the 0.9333 of a genuine abbreviation pair (`Michael Gordon` / `Michael R. Gordon`). A middle initial is two characters, so it barely moves a character-similarity score; raising the threshold drops true positives before it drops that pair. `test_conflicting_middle_initials_still_score_above_the_gate` pins the inversion, and fails if it ever reverses — at which point a threshold fix becomes viable and this rule can be revisited.

So discrimination comes from a second attribute. `corroborating_attributes()` reports which of **birth year** (±1) and **nationality** agree, requiring *both* sides to supply the attribute. A hit that publishes no birth date is not corroborated by our knowing one — it is unchecked, and reporting silence as agreement is exactly how the Liverpool FC false positive read as a finding.

Person signals are then capped when nothing but the name agrees — never `high`, since "high confidence this is the same person" is the one claim a name alone cannot support:

| | corroborated | name only |
|---|---|---|
| score ≥ 0.95 | `high` | `medium` |
| 0.88 ≤ score < 0.95 | `medium` | `low` |

The cap is two-tier rather than a flat `low` on purpose. Sanctions screening *is* name-based: an exact match against a designated person is something a reviewer must adjudicate, and filing every such hit under `low` would misreport standard practice as a weak lead. Either way the summary opens with **"Possible name match only"** and names what is missing, so the prose never asserts identity regardless of the rung. `evidence.corroboration` lists the agreeing attributes and `evidence.name_match_only` is the boolean.

**Entities keep the score-only ladder.** An organisation name is distinctive in a way a personal name is not — "Gazprom PJSC" matching "Gazprom" is a finding on its own.

The OpenAleph percolation screen shares all of this: it matches on names for the same reasons, so it imports `match_confidence()` / `match_summary()` rather than keeping a second copy. **BackgroundCheck (`/person-check`) follows the same rule since Phase 247.** It keeps its own strong/weak split for which hits produce signals at all, but a signal's confidence is now the lower of the rule's (a direct listing is `high`) and `match_confidence()`'s for the match, and an uncorroborated one opens "Possible name match only". Before, the same Jane Holl Lute record was `high` there with no birth year checked and `medium` on the company report. `evidence.match.corroboration` and `evidence.match.name_match_only` carry the inputs.

- `RELATED_PEP` — a related person matches an OpenSanctions PEP record or appears in EveryPolitician. The EveryPolitician summary names the record's own position ("PEP (Member of the Storting)"), or just "PEP" — never "political office-holder", which it used to say of employee-elected directors of state companies.
- `RELATED_PEP_SUBJECT_ROLE` (`kind: "context"`, Phase 247) — a related person who is a PEP **only by virtue of a role at the looked-up company itself**. See below.
- `RELATED_SANCTIONED` — a related person or entity matches an OpenSanctions record carrying `sanction`.
- `RELATED_COUNTER_SANCTIONED` — the match carries `sanction.counter`: a related party designated under a counter-sanctions regime. Emitted **last** in the ladder despite being a direct listing, so it is never the headline finding a consumer takes from `[0]`.
- `RELATED_SANCTIONS_CONTROLLED` — the match carries `sanction.control`: a related party inside a designated party's ownership chain.
- `RELATED_SANCTIONS_LINKED` — the match carries `sanction.linked` only: plain adjacency.
- `RELATED_DEBARMENT` — the match carries `debarment`.
- `RELATED_EXPORT_CONTROLLED` — the match carries `export.control`: a related party itself subject to export-control restrictions.
- `RELATED_EXPORT_CONTROL_LINKED` — the match carries `export.control.linked`: adjacent to an export-controlled party.
- `RELATED_EXPORT_RISK` — the match carries `export.risk`: flagged for trade risk.

These follow the **same reporting rule as the subject-level signals**: every fact a matched record asserts is reported, so a related party that is designated *and* inside another designated party's ownership chain surfaces both `RELATED_SANCTIONED` and `RELATED_SANCTIONS_CONTROLLED`. The one suppression is `RELATED_SANCTIONS_LINKED` when `RELATED_SANCTIONS_CONTROLLED` fires — upstream declares `sanction.linked` a superset of `sanction.control`, so the weaker code is the same fact stated less precisely rather than an additional one. Signals are ordered most-severe-first (direct listing → ownership chain → debarment → export-control listing → sanction adjacency → export adjacency → trade risk → PEP → counter-designation), so a consumer taking the first still gets the headline finding. The export-control rung sits above plain sanction adjacency because it is a restriction on the related party itself, and nothing within the export family is suppressed (no superset relationship upstream). `RELATED_COUNTER_SANCTIONED` sits at the end by design: it is structurally a direct listing, but by a regime the reader almost certainly owes no obligation to, so promoting it would misreport the finding. The OpenAleph percolation screen shares this rule, so the two cannot diverge.

### One chip per person per upstream record (Phase 247)

EveryPolitician is OpenSanctions' `peps` dataset read through the same API, and it returns OpenSanctions' own entity id. OpenAleph re-indexes OpenSanctions' collections and signs each id with its collection namespace — `Q28047991.d63cd770…` is OpenSanctions' `Q28047991`, a dot and 40 hex characters. So one PEP record reached the report up to three times, and again for each person statement carrying the name: Equinor drew **38** `RELATED_PEP` signals for **13** people.

`opencheck/pep_merge.py` merges them before `_merge_signals` counts anything. `upstream_record_id()` resolves each `RELATED_PEP` to the OpenSanctions id it is a copy of (a source whose `derived_from` names OpenSanctions; the OpenAleph signature stripped), and `merge_derived_pep()` keeps one signal per `(upstream record, normalised related-party name)`: the highest confidence, a tie going to the source the others are derived from. The others go to `evidence.also_reported_by` and the summary says "The same record is also reported by EveryPolitician and OpenAleph"; every person statement the group matched stays on `evidence.subject_statement_ids`, which `signalScope.ts` reads, so both of Anders Opedal's nodes keep the badge. Lineage decides here only whether two signals are *one observation*, never whether a signal fires. Two different upstream records with the same name stay two chips — they may be two people. Only `RELATED_PEP` is merged; the sanctions family keeps one signal per source.

### PEP by virtue of this role (Phase 247)

Most of Equinor's board are PEPs in OpenSanctions only through `no_brreg`, *Norway State-Owned Enterprises Leadership*: their one position is "Chairman, EQUINOR ASA" or "Board Member, EQUINOR ASA" (topic `gov.soe`). Screening a state company's board and reporting it politically exposed because it is the board of a state company is circular — true, and SOE leadership are PEPs under the EU regime, but not a finding about anyone's political role elsewhere.

`label_subject_role_peps()` reads each merged record's positions from OpenSanctions (`positionOccupancies[].post[]`, through the adapter's cached `fetch`; one call per distinct upstream record, at most 25). A position is at the subject when its `organization` is one of the subject's OpenSanctions records, or when the organisation its name carries — after the comma in Norway's "Chairman, EQUINOR ASA", before it in Denmark's "Ørsted A/S, board of directors (vice chairman)" — is one of the subject's names. When **every** position is at the subject, the signal becomes `RELATED_PEP_SUBJECT_ROLE`, `kind: "context"`, "listed as politically exposed on OpenSanctions only by virtue of a role at this company (Chairman, EQUINOR ASA)" — a slate chip labelled "PEP by virtue of this role", severity 0 on the graph, and never part of the verdict (Stephen, 25 Sept 2026). A record with another position (Haakon Bruun-Hanssen, Chief of Defence of Norway) stays `RELATED_PEP`, and its summary adds the role at this company. Evidence: `pep_role_checked`, `pep_positions`, `subject_role_positions`, `pep_by_subject_role`. A record that cannot be read changes nothing (`pep_role_checked: false`); it is a refinement of a finding that already stands, so it is not a degraded screen. On 25 Sept 2026's data: Equinor 38 → 13 rows, 12 labelled; Ørsted 7 rows, 6 labelled (Julia King, a member of the House of Lords, is not).

### The verdict says "possible" (Phase 247)

When every risk signal behind a name-match clause is `medium` or lower, the verdict uses the clause's *possible* form: "a possible politically exposed person among the parties named", likewise for related sanctions / export / debarment findings, offshore leaks and counter-sanctions. State ownership, watch-list jurisdictions and opacity keep one wording — their confidence is not about whether a record is the same person. `VERDICT_TEMPLATE` is 3, so the watchlist does not report the rewording as a change.

### OpenAleph per-collection collapse (Phase 119)

OpenAleph percolates against every percolator-indexed collection on the instance, so one designation-class fact about one related party used to surface once per collection carrying it — Igor Sechin's OFAC/UK/Japan/Ukraine listings rendered as four `RELATED_SANCTIONED` signals, and duplicate records inside one collection added more. That volume read as breadth: a reader infers N distinct findings from N chips. The screen now **collapses to one signal per `(code, subject_statement_id)`**: the survivor is the highest-(confidence, score) copy, every copy is preserved in `evidence.collections` (label, URL, matched name, hit id, score), `evidence.collection_count` counts distinct collections, `corroboration` is the union across copies, and a multi-collection summary reads "listed across N OpenAleph collections" — deliberately never "corroborated", because aggregation copies inside one instance are not independent sources. Because `/signalstats` counts post-dedup inside `_merge_signals`, the instrument reports the collapsed number automatically. Cross-source duplicates (OpenSanctions + OpenAleph for the same person) are untouched by this collapse, which never crosses `source_id`; for `RELATED_PEP` the Phase 247 merge above does cross it, on the upstream record id.

Two match guards also apply to entity targets on this screen (both sides of the comparison): the name must keep a non-empty residue once legal-form tokens are stripped (`names.org_name_residue`). A record named only «Общество с ограниченной ответственностью» — "Limited Liability Company" in Russian — cannot be meaningfully matched by name; in production such a record matched nineteen distinct subsidiaries at 0.88+ on legal-form similarity alone.

The normaliser folds standalone non-ASCII letters (Polish `ł`, Norwegian `ø`, German `ß`, Icelandic `ð`/`þ`, French `œ`) so transliterated and native spellings match. Bounded at `max_targets=25` distinct related parties per lookup to keep the OpenSanctions request volume sane on large groups. **Which 25 is chosen, and a cap that bites is reported (Phase 279, `related_targets.py`).** Targets are first deduped across sources — the same kind, and a shared identifier, or an identical normalised name whose birth years (founding years for entities; entities also jurisdictions) do not conflict, a year missing on one side not being a conflict — so Wikidata's and OpenCorporates' copies of one director are one party, screened once. A person's name is compared order- and honorific-insensitively (Phase 282: Companies House's "FIORE, Norman Benito" and OpenCorporates' "NORMAN BENITO FIORE" are one director). A signal the party earns is **one signal per upstream record**, anchored on the party's first statement and naming every statement of the cluster in `evidence.subject_statement_ids`, so each source's card keeps its badge (Phase 282; under Phase 279 each statement got its own copy, and one party counted once per source that named it — Moody's Corporation was two `RELATED_EXPORT_RISK` signals on Risk First Limited). A cluster joined by an identifier can hold different spellings (GLEIF's Cyrillic legal name, OpenSanctions' English one), and a name screen matches on the spelling, so each distinct normalised name is read — up to three per party — while the party counts once against the limit. They are then ranked: current before former (a cluster is former only when every member is); within each, owners and controllers (`beneficialOwnershipOrControl`, or a shareholding/voting/control/arrangement interest on a current relationship), then officers, then everyone else; within each tier a party linked directly to the looked-up company before one linked further out; among officers equally close, board chair, then board members, then senior managing officials (Phase 282). **Ties never fall to bundle order** (Phase 282): bundle order is the order the sources answered, so it changed between runs and with it which parties at the limit were screened (Eli Lilly, 2 Oct 2026, had twelve current owners and officers past the limit and a different verdict on two runs). Among parties still tied, one carrying an LEI comes first (Phase 284): a GLEIF-registered group member before an officer's other appointments — when a large group shares one tier, plain name order picked an arbitrary slice, and on Taqa Bratani (93 parties, all in the bottom tier) it pushed Taweelah Asia Power Company, the group member carrying the sanctions-controlled and export-control-linked findings, past the limit. Remaining ties go to the party more sources name, then by name key, birth/founding year and identifiers — never statementIds, which change between runs — and the representative spelling of a party is chosen the same way. When parties are left unread, the screen appends a `DegradedSource` with `source_id: "opencheck"`, `check: "cross_source_names"`, `reason: "truncated"` and a counts-only detail ("covered the 25 highest-ranked of 49 related parties; 24 were not screened (10 former; 14 current with no ownership or board role recorded)"). The verdict then says the check "answered only in part — not every related party was screened", not that it did not run, and the notice offers no re-run, because the same bundle selects the same parties. Before Phase 279 the first 25 in bundle order were read and the rest dropped silently, so one more source answering could push a real party out: Eli Lilly's `RELATED_PEP` came and went between runs, and Rosneft screened 25 of 106 with Igor Sechin at position 104. The cross-check is a no-op when live mode is off or no OpenSanctions API key is configured.

## ICIJ Offshore Leaks name cross-check

For every `personStatement` and `entityStatement` in the assembled BODS bundle, OpenCheck posts each name to the [ICIJ Offshore Leaks reconciliation API](https://offshoreleaks.icij.org/docs/reconciliation) in batches of 8 names × 3 node types (Entity, Officer, Intermediary — Address is excluded by construction: measured live, address nodes took 57.8% of every result set and produced zero signals while displacing real matches). The API covers roughly 800,000 offshore entities and associated individuals across the Panama Papers, Paradise Papers, Pandora Papers, Bahamas Leaks, and the original Offshore Leaks dataset.

- `OFFSHORE_LEAKS` — a name matches an ICIJ Offshore Leaks record. ICIJ's own score (≥ 70) is a first filter and its `match: true` flag decides nothing (Phase 237). Confidence comes from the gates below: an **entity** match is `high` when the party's country is among the countries ICIJ holds for the node, else `medium`; a **person** match is `medium` when so corroborated, else `low` (Phase 282 — ICIJ person nodes carry no birth date, so the country is the only corroborator, and without it a common name such as "Christopher Jones" matches on spelling alone). Intermediary matches are worded as the different finding they are (the law firm or formation agent that *arranged* a structure).

Three name checks stand between an ICIJ result and a signal, because ICIJ's own score cannot be trusted alone (it rated a known false pair 90/100):

1. **Name similarity ≥ 0.87** using the shared Phase-D scorer (`names.name_similarity`) — re-measured for this surface in Phase 120 (down from PR #86's 0.93, whose cut cost two adjudicated true matches at 0.877–0.878).
2. **Distinctive-token agreement** (`names.distinctive_token_agreement`, Phase 120) for entity targets — and for person-kind targets whose name carries a legal form (corporate officers filed as persons). Legal forms are stripped, filler tokens (HOLDINGS, INTERNATIONAL, GROUP…) dropped, and the remaining distinctive residues must agree: numeric discriminators identical on both sides ("HORNSEA 1" never matches "HORNSEA"), every token of the shorter residue matching the longer exactly or within a one-edit typo budget (tokens ≥4 chars). An empty residue never agrees. This kills the collisions no threshold could ("CASTROL Holdings International" vs "COSCO International Holdings" scores 0.92 on shared filler alone) while letting the threshold recover true matches below the old cut. Full measurement: [ICIJ distinctive-token evaluation](icij-distinctive-token-evaluation.md).
3. **Person token agreement** (`names.person_token_agreement`, Phase 282) for every other person target. Honorifics are stripped (rigour's `remove_person_prefixes`) — before the similarity check too, because "Mr." on both sides lifted "Robert Frederick Smith" vs "Robert Frederick White" from 0.864 to 0.880 — and every name token of the shorter name must agree with a token of the longer, exactly or within rigour's default edit budget (≤20%, tokens ≥4 chars; "Mohammed"/"Muhammad" agree, "Smith"/"White" do not). Initials are ignored and word order does not matter, so "JONES, Christopher Paul" agrees with "CHRISTOPHER JONES" and "FIORE, Norman Benito" with "NORMAN BENITO FIORE".

Then the Phase 237 gates on the node's details (ICIJ's `extend` service): the **date** gate drops a party founded or born after the leak's documents end, and the **jurisdiction** gate compares the party's countries — an entity's `jurisdiction` and address countries, a person's address countries **except service addresses** (Phase 282: a Companies House officer's only address is a service address, usually the company's own office, so it says where the company is, not the person) — with the node's `country_codes`. Countries on both sides with none in common is a recorded mismatch, and **the match is dropped** (Phase 282; before, it only annotated, and a GB director matched an Italy-only Pandora Papers record at medium). Each signal's `evidence.gates` says which gates passed and why the match stands at its confidence.

Signals are scoped to the matching BODS `statementId` (in `evidence.subject_statement_id`) — the same deduplication logic as `RELATED_PEP` / `RELATED_SANCTIONED`. **The subject's own names are screened once, as the subject (Phase 235):** up to three distinct spellings across the subject identity set (GLEIF's legal name, OpenSanctions' English one…), each attributed to one statement — GLEIF's — so a match is one signal however many sources spell the name the same way. Such a signal carries `evidence.statement_id` and `evidence.subject: true` (not `subject_statement_id`), and reads "The looked-up company's name '…' matches a record in …" — still a name match, never an identity claim. A minimum-specificity guard (`_screenable`) drops names whose comparable form is under 3 characters. No API key is required; the check runs in live mode automatically. Bounded at `max_targets=30` related parties, selected and reported exactly as for the cross-check above (Phase 279: dedupe, rank, `truncated` degradation with `check: "icij_offshore_leaks"`); the subject's own names sit outside the limit.
